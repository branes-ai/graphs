Mission dossier · kpu_t128_n7 on tsmc_n7

Drone: ISR (endurance / wide-area search)

Companion-annex far-flight regime: onboard 2 B VLM, emissions-restricted. 18 stages, 25 W, 200 ms. What has to be true of the CPU and the accelerator for that to close.

This design does not serve this mission. It is short of 3,716 tiles against 128, 29 cores against 12 and 306 GB/s of memory bandwidth against 204.8. Three stages cannot be priced at all, so the engine and power figures here are floors -- the memory figure is not, since it counts every stage's bytes.

The shortfall has two owners: vlm is 99.9% of the KPU demand and tsdf is 64.1% of the CPU demand.

1. The use case

A small drone searching wide areas for a long time, emissions-restricted: it cannot radio home for help, so whatever interprets what it sees has to run on the airframe. That is why there is a 2-billion-parameter vision-language model in the loop at 2 queries a second, and it is the whole reason this mission looks the way it does.

Everything else is modest. 2 cameras at 20 Hz, one stereo pair, one radar, a 200 ms deadline that is generous for the speeds involved, and 25 W to fly on. The interesting comparison is the interceptor: the same airframe class and the same power budget, flown at the opposite time constant, with no VLM aboard.

2 x 1440x1080 streams at 20 Hz. 18 stages over 7 tiers, 11 of them on the sense-to-act chain that the 200 ms deadline applies to.

3. The workload

18 stages, from the sensor front end through state estimation, mapping and semantics to reactive control and actuation. They do not run at one rate: from 2 /s to 62.2 M/s in their own units, which is why each box carries what it demands of an engine rather than a share of some common frame.

The arithmetic is concentrated. vlm alone is 54% of the operations. The bytes are concentrated somewhere else: vlm is 85% of the traffic. Those two facts pull the design in different directions, and section 6 is about which one wins.

5. The configuration

One KPU fabric, a cluster of Andes RISC-V cores, a shared fabric and one LPDDR5 interface at tsmc_n7. The capacities below — how many cores and tiles exist — come from the selected design and are an input. What section 6 produces is how many the mission needs, which is the number to compare them against.

The CPU block carries baseline figures: we have measured an Arm Cortex-A78AE, not an Andes core. They stand in for the socket until you replace them, which is what section 6 asks for. Nothing here is a claim about Andes silicon.

Three numbers per compute block, because any two mislead: X, the throughput one server actually delivers; U, the share of wall clock it is busy; E, the ops-weighted mean of the per-class efficiencies it runs at. X and U compose exactly — demand = X × servers × U — while E says how well the kernels suit the machine. Where a stage needs many servers at a low ceiling and another needs few at a high one, E and X/peak diverge, and E is the one that describes the kernels rather than the sizing.

A U above 100% is not a sizing. It is the design being asked for more than it has, and the figure beside it says by how much.

2. Size each engine

KPU. 4 stages need 3,716 tiles between them, against the 128 this design has. vlm alone is 99.9% of that, at an efficiency of 0.14%.

CPU. 11 stages need 29 cores between them, against the 12 this design has. tsdf alone is 64.1% of that, at an efficiency of 0.34%.

3. What binds

  • the KPU: 2903% — over capacity.
  • the CPU: 242% — over capacity.
  • memory bandwidth: 149% — over capacity.
  • datapath power (arithmetic only): 8% of what is there.

Memory is the one constraint here the parts list can answer. The mission asks 306 GB/s and this design supplies 204.8. On bandwidth alone, lpddr5x_phy_512b at 546.1 GB/s would carry it at 56% and hbm3_1stack at 819.2 GB/s would carry it at 37%. These are bandwidth-qualified candidates and nothing more: a wider LPDDR interface changes the PHY and the floorplan, and an HBM stack changes the controller, the package and the thermal design as well. Whether either is compatible with this SoC is a question this model does not answer. What it does say is that the shortfall is within reach of parts we already catalogue, which is not true of either engine.

Most of it is one stage: vlm at 85% of the traffic — at 2 Hz, a 130 GB working set pulled through DRAM on every call.

4. The CPU ask

The CPU needs 29 cores and the design has 12. The gap is not spread evenly: three of the 11 stages on it are 84% of the demand.

  • tsdf (raycast): 19 cores at 0.34% of FP32 peak.
  • esdf (wavefront): 3.73 cores at 2.97% of FP32 peak.
  • gain (raycast): 2.18 cores at 0.34% of FP32 peak.

Grouped by kernel class, the answer is blunter still: raycast is 21 of the 29 cores (72%) at 0.34% of peak. So the question for an Andes core is not a general one:

What does it take to run 2.49 GOP/s of raycast in FP32 at materially better than 0.34% of peak?

The leverage is enormous because the baseline is so low. At 10% of peak instead of 0.34%, that class falls from 21 cores to 0.706, and the whole CPU requirement from 29 to 8.93 — 11 cores provisioned instead of 12.

5. The accelerator ask is a memory ask

vlm is 99.9% of the 3,716-tile requirement, and not because it is the biggest arithmetic: it is 54% of the operations. It is because its domain-flow ceiling is 0.14%. The fabric is not computing, it is waiting: 130 GB of weights crossing DRAM on each of its 2 calls a second.

Take it out and the rest of the accelerator work — det, sdfenc, policy — needs 3.59 tiles. That is the shape of the decision: this is not a mission that needs a 3,716-tile fabric, it is a mission with a weight-streaming problem bolted to a 3.59-tile one. Quantisation, weight caching in on-die SRAM, a smaller model or a wider memory interface are all attacks on the same number; more tiles is not.

The safety path

Sizing answers a rate: can the machine do this much work per second. A safety argument needs a deadline: does each loop on the sense-to-act chain close inside its own period. Those are different questions, and this page answers only the first. The table below is the closest it gets to the second.

The filter closes; an input it reads does not. The 50 Hz cbf filter takes 125 us of its 20 ms period and needs 0.00627 of a core. That part is comfortable. What is not is esdf at 3.73x its period.

cbf reads its hazards out of the ESDF, so the filter is correct arithmetic over a distance field the machine cannot refresh at the rate the mission asks for. One update takes 3.73x its own period — that is a service-time-to-period ratio, not the age of the data. How old the hazards actually are depends on whether late updates queue or are dropped, which is a scheduling policy this model does not carry, so the age is unbounded here rather than 3.73x.

A server count does not fix it. Its unit is per map update: the call is the loop iteration, so k servers carry k overlapping iterations rather than one faster one. That is a latency result, not a throughput result.

Two stages on the chain have no figure at all: sgm and vio. Nothing prices them on either engine, so they contribute no time to the table above. The chain's true latency is longer than anything here by an unknown amount, which is why this page quotes no end-to-end number.

What this model cannot tell you

Everything below is required for a safety case and absent here. None of it is a gap we can close by computing harder; each needs evidence this repository does not hold.

  • Worst-case execution time. Every figure on this page is a mean rate from measured throughput. A deadline is met or missed in the tail, and we model no tail: no cache behaviour, no contention, no interrupt latency, no scheduler.
  • Freedom from interference. The chain shares one memory interface with everything else, and the largest other consumers are vlm at 260 GB/s and det at 7.67 GB/s — 131% of the interface between them. Nothing here bounds what that contention does to a period on the chain.
  • The safety island cannot be sized. The design carries one (island_sram anchored, lockstep_r52_cores not), but the catalogue states no compute capability for it, so no stage can be placed on it and this page cannot tell you what could run there instead.
  • Integrity level, fault coverage, diagnostic coverage, FIT rates and safe-state transition time. The catalogue carries none of these, so this page makes no claim about any standard, and its numbers cannot be cited towards one.

Read the table as a necessary condition, not a sufficient one, and read it against the efficiencies in it. A loop that does not close here does not close at these efficiencies — a faster implementation of the same kernel could close it, which is exactly what section 6 asks for. A loop that closes here has passed only the easiest of the tests it has to pass.

What this rests on

Four kinds of figure, and the page says which each is:

  • Catalogue. The mission, its rates, per-call operation and byte counts, the SoC composition, the node's energy per operation.
  • Measured. CPU efficiencies from orin_nano_measured_v1, with the run behind each figure cited in the table. These are measured on Arm Cortex-A78AE cores in a Jetson Orin Nano — our reference baseline, not a claim about any other core. They are why the CPU requirement is stated as a throughput target rather than a core count.
  • Ceiling. KPU efficiencies are our domain-flow upper bound, taken from the smallest core with a stated schedule (kpu_t64_core). A ceiling bounds, it does not predict, so every tile count is a lower bound.
  • Gap. Named, never filled.

Area. A least-squares line through our four catalogued N7 cores gives 0.1665 mm² per tile plus 0.287 mm² fixed, so a one-tile fabric is about 0.45 mm². The CPU side we cannot price at all: we anchor the cluster's caches but not its core logic, because no absolute core area is published for the baseline. An Andes core area would close that column outright.
