Mission dossier · kpu_t128_n7 on tsmc_n7

Autonomous vehicle: SAE L4 / L5 (high / full automation)

No fallback driver. 20+ cameras to 8 MP, 3-5 LiDAR, 8 radars, dual-redundant stack. 18 stages, 800 W, 100 ms. What has to be true of the CPU and the accelerator for that to close.

This design does not serve this mission. It is short of 1,575 tiles against 128, 254 cores against 12 and 386 GB/s of memory bandwidth against 204.8. Three stages cannot be priced at all, so the engine and power figures here are floors -- the memory figure is not, since it counts every stage's bytes.

The shortfall has two owners: vlm is 98.9% of the KPU demand and tsdf is 48.7% of the CPU demand.

1. The use case

A vehicle with no fallback driver. Not a driver-assist system with a human watching it — the stack is the driver, and there is nobody to hand back to. The sensor suite says as much: 20 cameras, 8 radars, lidar, and a dual-redundant stack behind them.

That is the whole difference from the level below. L3 assumes a human who takes over when the system gives up; this removes that human, and everything the human was implicitly covering has to be computed instead.

This profile covers L4 and L5 together, and they are not the same problem. L4 is bounded by an operational design domain — a geofence, a weather envelope, a road class — and everything outside it is a reason to stop rather than a case to handle. L5 has no such boundary.

The sensor suite and rates stated here describe an L4-class build, so read the page in three parts. The demand figures — operations, bytes, cores and tiles needed — are a lower bound for L5, by an amount the catalogue does not state. The deadline and power budget are this profile's own targets and are not L5 requirements; whether an L5 platform's envelope differs at all is not something we hold. The engine capacities are inputs from the selected design and say nothing about either level. Separating the two needs an L5 workload with sourced rates, which we do not have (graphs#339).

20 x 2896x1876 streams at 30 Hz. 18 stages over 7 tiers, 11 of them on the sense-to-act chain that the 100 ms deadline applies to.

3. The workload

18 stages, from the sensor front end through state estimation, mapping and semantics to reactive control and actuation. They do not run at one rate: from 0.25 /s to 3.26 G/s in their own units, which is why each box carries what it demands of an engine rather than a share of some common frame.

The arithmetic is concentrated. det alone is 74% of the operations. The bytes are concentrated somewhere else: sgm is 38% of the traffic. Those two facts pull the design in different directions, and section 6 is about which one wins.

5. The configuration

One KPU fabric, a cluster of Andes RISC-V cores, a shared fabric and one LPDDR5 interface at tsmc_n7. The capacities below — how many cores and tiles exist — come from the selected design and are an input. What section 6 produces is how many the mission needs, which is the number to compare them against.

The CPU block carries baseline figures: we have measured an Arm Cortex-A78AE, not an Andes core. They stand in for the socket until you replace them, which is what section 6 asks for. Nothing here is a claim about Andes silicon.

Three numbers per compute block, because any two mislead: X, the throughput one server actually delivers; U, the share of wall clock it is busy; E, the ops-weighted mean of the per-class efficiencies it runs at. X and U compose exactly — demand = X × servers × U — while E says how well the kernels suit the machine. Where a stage needs many servers at a low ceiling and another needs few at a high one, E and X/peak diverge, and E is the one that describes the kernels rather than the sizing.

A U above 100% is not a sizing. It is the design being asked for more than it has, and the figure beside it says by how much.

2. Size each engine

KPU. 4 stages need 1,575 tiles between them, against the 128 this design has. vlm alone is 98.9% of that, at an efficiency of 0.14%.

CPU. 11 stages need 254 cores between them, against the 12 this design has. tsdf alone is 48.7% of that, at an efficiency of 0.34%.

3. What binds

  • the CPU: 2119% — over capacity.
  • the KPU: 1230% — over capacity.
  • memory bandwidth: 188% — over capacity.
  • datapath power (arithmetic only): 1% of what is there.

Memory is the one constraint here the parts list can answer. The mission asks 386 GB/s and this design supplies 204.8. On bandwidth alone, lpddr5x_phy_512b at 546.1 GB/s would carry it at 71% and hbm3_1stack at 819.2 GB/s would carry it at 47%. These are bandwidth-qualified candidates and nothing more: a wider LPDDR interface changes the PHY and the floorplan, and an HBM stack changes the controller, the package and the thermal design as well. Whether either is compatible with this SoC is a question this model does not answer. What it does say is that the shortfall is within reach of parts we already catalogue, which is not true of either engine.

Note what that traffic is made of: 52% of it comes from stages no engine can price — sgm, radar and vio. Bytes are counted whether or not an engine can run the work, so the memory figure is complete where the engine figures are floors, and on this mission the majority of the memory demand comes from the stages the compute numbers leave out.

The largest single contributor is sgm at 38% of the traffic — at 60 Hz, a 2.44 GB working set pulled through DRAM on every call.

4. The CPU ask

The CPU needs 254 cores and the design has 12. The gap is not spread evenly: three of the 11 stages on it are 93% of the demand.

  • tsdf (raycast): 124 cores at 0.34% of FP32 peak.
  • mono (pixel_fixed_function): 83 cores at 4.48% of FP32 peak.
  • esdf (wavefront): 30 cores at 2.97% of FP32 peak.

Grouped by kernel class, the answer is blunter still: raycast is 126 of the 254 cores (49%) at 0.34% of peak. So the question for an Andes core is not a general one:

What does it take to run 15 GOP/s of raycast in FP32 at materially better than 0.34% of peak?

The leverage is enormous because the baseline is so low. At 10% of peak instead of 0.34%, that class falls from 126 cores to 4.28, and the whole CPU requirement from 254 to 133 — 157 cores provisioned instead of 12.

5. The accelerator ask is a memory ask

vlm is 98.9% of the 1,575-tile requirement, and not because it is the biggest arithmetic: it is 9% of the operations. It is because its domain-flow ceiling is 0.14%. The fabric is not computing, it is waiting: 455 GB of weights crossing DRAM every 4 seconds.

Take it out and the rest of the accelerator work — det, sdfenc, policy — needs 17 tiles. That is the shape of the decision: this is not a mission that needs a 1,575-tile fabric, it is a mission with a weight-streaming problem bolted to a 17-tile one. Quantisation, weight caching in on-die SRAM, a smaller model or a wider memory interface are all attacks on the same number; more tiles is not.

The filter closes; an input it reads does not. The 400 Hz cbf filter takes 161 us of its 2.5 ms period and needs 0.0644 of a core. That part is comfortable. What is not is esdf at 29.8x its period.

cbf reads its hazards out of the ESDF, so at these rates it is correct arithmetic over a distance field that is 29.8x out of date.

A server count does not fix it. Its unit is per map update: the call is the loop iteration, so k servers carry k overlapping iterations rather than one faster one. That is a latency result, not a throughput result.

Two stages on the chain have no figure at all: sgm and vio. Nothing prices them on either engine, so they contribute no time to the table above. The chain's true latency is longer than anything here by an unknown amount, which is why this page quotes no end-to-end number.

What this model cannot tell you

Everything below is required for a safety case and absent here. None of it is a gap we can close by computing harder; each needs evidence this repository does not hold.

  • Worst-case execution time. Every figure on this page is a mean rate from measured throughput. A deadline is met or missed in the tail, and we model no tail: no cache behaviour, no contention, no interrupt latency, no scheduler.
  • Freedom from interference. The chain shares one memory interface with everything else, and the largest other consumers are vlm at 114 GB/s and radar at 48.5 GB/s — 79% of the interface between them. Nothing here bounds what that contention does to a period on the chain.
  • The safety island cannot be sized. The design carries one (island_sram anchored, lockstep_r52_cores not), but the catalogue states no compute capability for it, so no stage can be placed on it and this page cannot tell you what could run there instead.
  • Integrity level, fault coverage, diagnostic coverage, FIT rates and safe-state transition time. The catalogue carries none of these, so this page makes no claim about any standard, and its numbers cannot be cited towards one.

Read the table as a necessary condition, not a sufficient one, and read it against the efficiencies in it. A loop that does not close here does not close at these efficiencies — a faster implementation of the same kernel could close it, which is exactly what section 6 asks for. A loop that closes here has passed only the easiest of the tests it has to pass.

What this rests on

Four kinds of figure, and the page says which each is:

  • Catalogue. The mission, its rates, per-call operation and byte counts, the SoC composition, the node's energy per operation.
  • Measured. CPU efficiencies from orin_nano_measured_v1, with the run behind each figure cited in the table. These are measured on Arm Cortex-A78AE cores in a Jetson Orin Nano — our reference baseline, not a claim about any other core. They are why the CPU requirement is stated as a throughput target rather than a core count.
  • Ceiling. KPU efficiencies are our domain-flow upper bound, taken from the smallest core with a stated schedule (kpu_t64_core). A ceiling bounds, it does not predict, so every tile count is a lower bound.
  • Gap. Named, never filled.

Area. A least-squares line through our four catalogued N7 cores gives 0.1665 mm² per tile plus 0.287 mm² fixed, so a one-tile fabric is about 0.45 mm². The CPU side we cannot price at all: we anchor the cluster's caches but not its core logic, because no absolute core area is published for the baseline. An Andes core area would close that column outright.
