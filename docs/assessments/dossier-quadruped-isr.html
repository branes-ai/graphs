Mission dossier · kpu_t128_n7 on tsmc_n7

Quadruped: ISR (dismounted, comms-denied)

Unstructured terrain, onboard 2 B VLM, no datalink assumption. 18 stages, 60 W, 150 ms. What has to be true of the CPU and the accelerator for that to close.

Nothing in this parts list serves this mission. It needs 1,862 tiles against the 128 this design has; 36 cores against the 12 this design has. It asks 198 GB/s of a 204.8 GB/s memory interface, 97% of it. Three stages cannot be priced at all, so every one of those figures is a floor.

That is not one problem but three, and they have different owners: vlm is 99.7% of the KPU demand, tsdf is 51.4% of the CPU demand, vlm alone is 64% of the memory interface.

1. The use case

A legged robot carrying ISR for a dismounted team, over unstructured terrain, with no datalink to lean on. Everything runs on the animal: stereo and lidar for terrain it has never seen, visual-inertial odometry because GPS is not assumed, a detector and a 2-billion-parameter vision-language model for what it is looking at, and a full reactive control stack so it stays upright while doing all of it.

Comms-denied is the whole constraint. There is no cloud to hand the VLM to, no operator in the loop, and a 60 W budget that has to cover the compute alongside the actuators.

4 x 1280x720 streams at 30 Hz. 18 stages over 7 tiers, 11 of them on the sense-to-act chain that the 150 ms deadline applies to.

3. The workload

18 stages, from the sensor front end through state estimation, mapping and semantics to reactive control and actuation. They do not run at one rate: from 1 /s to 111 M/s in their own units, which is why each box carries what it demands of an engine rather than a share of some common frame.

The arithmetic is concentrated. det alone is 60% of the operations. The bytes are concentrated somewhere else: vlm is 66% of the traffic. Those two facts pull the design in different directions, and section 6 is about which one wins.

5. The configuration

One KPU fabric, a cluster of Andes RISC-V cores, a shared fabric and one LPDDR5 interface at tsmc_n7. Engine counts are the output of section 6, not an input.

The CPU block carries baseline figures: we have measured an Arm Cortex-A78AE, not an Andes core. They stand in for the socket until you replace them, which is what section 6 asks for. Nothing here is a claim about Andes silicon.

Three numbers per compute block, because any two mislead: X, the throughput one server actually delivers; U, the share of wall clock it is busy; E, the share of its dense peak that X represents. They compose exactly — demand = X × servers × U.

A U above 100% is not a sizing. It is the design being asked for more than it has, and the figure beside it says by how much.

2. Size each engine

KPU. 4 stages need 1,862 tiles between them, against the 128 this design has. vlm alone is 99.7% of that, at an efficiency of 0.14%.

CPU. 11 stages need 36 cores between them, against the 12 this design has. tsdf alone is 51.4% of that, at an efficiency of 0.34%.

3. What binds

  • the KPU: 1455% — over capacity.
  • the CPU: 301% — over capacity.
  • memory bandwidth: 97% of what is there.
  • power: 3% of what is there.

Memory is worth spelling out. The interface carries 198 GB/s of compulsory traffic against 204.8 GB/s of peak, and vlm is 64% of the interface on its own — at 1 Hz. That is a 130 GB working set pulled through DRAM on every call.

4. The CPU ask

The CPU needs 36 cores and the design has 12. The gap is not spread evenly: three of the 11 stages on it are 83% of the demand.

  • tsdf (raycast): 19 cores at 0.34% of FP32 peak.
  • esdf (wavefront): 5.97 cores at 2.97% of FP32 peak.
  • gain (raycast): 5.44 cores at 0.34% of FP32 peak.

Grouped by kernel class, the answer is blunter still: raycast is 24 of the 36 cores (66%) at 0.34% of peak. So the question for an Andes core is not a general one:

What does it take to run 2.88 GOP/s of raycast in FP32 at materially better than 0.34% of peak?

The leverage is enormous because the baseline is so low. At 10% of peak instead of 0.34%, that class falls from 24 cores to 0.817, and the whole CPU requirement from 36 to 13 — 16 cores provisioned instead of 12.

5. The accelerator ask is a memory ask

vlm is 99.7% of the 1,862-tile requirement, and not because it is the biggest arithmetic: it is 27% of the operations. It is because its domain-flow ceiling is 0.14%. The fabric is not computing, it is waiting: 130 GB of weights crossing DRAM once a second.

Take it out and the rest of the accelerator work — det, sdfenc, policy — needs 5.7 tiles. That is the shape of the decision: this is not a mission that needs a 1,862-tile fabric, it is a mission with a weight-streaming problem bolted to a 5.7-tile one. Quantisation, weight caching in on-die SRAM, a smaller model or a wider memory interface are all attacks on the same number; more tiles is not.

7. What this rests on

Four kinds of figure, and the page says which each is:

  • Catalogue. The mission, its rates, per-call operation and byte counts, the SoC composition, the node's energy per operation.
  • Measured. CPU efficiencies from orin_nano_measured_v1, with the run behind each figure cited in the table. These are measured on Arm Cortex-A78AE cores in a Jetson Orin Nano — our reference baseline, not a claim about any other core. They are why the CPU requirement is stated as a throughput target rather than a core count.
  • Ceiling. KPU efficiencies are our domain-flow upper bound, taken from the smallest core with a stated schedule (kpu_t64_core). A ceiling bounds, it does not predict, so every tile count is a lower bound.
  • Gap. Named, never filled.

Area. A least-squares line through our four catalogued N7 cores gives 0.1665 mm² per tile plus 0.287 mm² fixed, so a one-tile fabric is about 0.45 mm². The CPU side we cannot price at all: we anchor the cluster's caches but not its core logic, because no absolute core area is published for the baseline. An Andes core area would close that column outright.
