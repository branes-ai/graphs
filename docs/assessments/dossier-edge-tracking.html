Edge AI device: Multi-stream tracking & anomaly

Four 1080p camera streams at 15 Hz, an open-vocabulary detector on every frame, and a 5 W budget. This is the whole sizing argument for that product on a CPU + KPU SoC: what the workload demands, what each engine delivers, and how many cores and tiles it takes before the demand fits.

The answer is 4 CPU cores and 1 KPU tile. The detector — 99.2% of the mission's arithmetic — needs 0.48 of a tile. The camera light path, 0.8% of the arithmetic, needs 3.16 CPU cores. Neither compute engine is the binding constraint: DRAM runs at 2.2% of peak, the datapath draws 113 mW against a 5 W budget, and the chain finishes in 21.1 ms against a 200 ms deadline. What binds is a 4.5% efficiency on the pixel stage, and that is an argument about the CPU, not about the accelerator.

1. The use case

A fixed-install edge appliance watching four camera streams — a loading bay, a retail floor, a perimeter. It runs an open-vocabulary detector on every frame of every stream, keeps identities across frames, and raises an anomaly when something does not belong. It is mains- or PoE-powered, fanless, and it is expected to run unattended for years.

The catalogue states it as: Four 1080p streams at 15 Hz, small detector plus re-identification. In numbers, that is 4 x 1920x1080 streams at 15 Hz and 60 detector inferences per second, which is one per frame across all four streams.

Two properties of this use case drive everything that follows. First, the detector is not latency-critical but is throughput-critical: a 200 ms deadline is generous for a 60 Hz aggregate frame rate, but every frame must be processed, so the sizing question is one of sustained rate, not of tail latency. Second, the pixel path and the detector are completely different kinds of work, and the analysis in section 6 turns on exactly that.

3. The workload

The pipeline is two stages, and its shape is the reason the sizing works out the way it does. Raw sensor data enters the light path, which is a per-pixel fixed-function chain; the detector then runs once per frame on the result. Everything else a tracking product does — association, re-identification, the anomaly rule — is folded into the detector's per-call cost by the catalogue, which anchors it to a published FLOP count rather than modelling it separately.

5. The configuration

The configuration is one KPU fabric, a cluster of Cortex-A78AE-class CPU cores, a shared on-chip fabric and one LPDDR5 interface, all at tsmc_n7. The diagram shows it as sized — the engine counts are the output of section 6, not an input.

Three numbers describe each compute block, and any two of them mislead:

  • X, the throughput one server actually delivers while it is running this work.
  • U, the share of wall clock that server is busy — the sizing margin.
  • E, the share of the server's dense peak that X represents — how well the kernel suits the machine.

They compose exactly: demand = X × servers × U. A block with high E and low U is oversized. A block with high U and low E is not oversized; it is badly matched, and buying more of it is the expensive way out.

Read the two compute blocks against each other. The KPU delivers 1.3 TOP/s per tile at E = 97.5% of its dense peak. The CPU delivers 1.58 GOP/s per core at E = 4.5%. That is a factor of 22 in how well each engine is suited to the work it was given — and the engine doing badly is the one carrying less than one percent of the operations.

6. The analysis: dimensioning the engines

Step 1: each stage has exactly one engine that can take it

Before any sizing, the mapping is forced. A precision class runs in the narrowest format the engine offers at or above the class's floor, and the efficiency of that (kernel class, engine, format) triple has to come from somewhere — a measurement, or the domain-flow ceiling. Where it does not exist, the stage does not get a number, and nothing is substituted for it.

  • mono is pixel_fixed_function, entirely class C, so it runs in FP32. On the KPU there is no fit: the domain-flow model has no schedule for that kernel class on a regular wavefront fabric, so there is no ceiling to quote and nothing has measured one. It goes to the CPU, where FP32 is measured.
  • det is dense_conv_gemm, 85% class A and 15% class B. On the CPU, class A binds to INT8 — the narrowest format the CPU offers at or above A's floor — and nothing measures INT8 dense_conv_gemm on a CPU, so there is no fit. It goes to the KPU, where the domain-flow model does have a schedule.

Neither placement is a preference. The table below is the whole of it: two stages, two engines, and exactly one number in each row.

Step 2: size each engine to its own stage

The detector. At a ceiling efficiency of 97.5%, one tile delivers 1.3 TOP/s. The detector asks for 620 GOP/s, so it needs 0.476 of a tile. One tile covers it at 47.6% utilization. Because that efficiency is a ceiling rather than a measurement, the honest reading is no fewer than 0.48 tiles.

The light path. At a measured efficiency of 4.48%, one core delivers 1.58 GOP/s against a 35.2 GOP/s FP32 peak. The light path asks for 4.98 GOP/s, so it needs 3.156 cores. Four cores cover it at 78.9%.

Step 3: check what is not the constraint

Three candidates fail to bind, and it is worth being explicit about each:

  • Memory bandwidth. 4.43 GB/s of compulsory traffic against 204.8 GB/s of peak — 2.2%. The narrowest interface in the catalogue is roughly 46x oversized for this mission.
  • Energy. The datapath floor is 113 mW against a 5 W budget: 2.3%. Even if the rest of the SoC costs an order of magnitude more than its arithmetic, the budget holds.
  • Latency. 21.1 ms serial against 200 ms: 9.5x of margin, before pipelining the two stages.

Step 4: what the KPU is worth here

The detector has no fit on the CPU because nobody has measured INT8 dense_conv_gemm there. But class A is INT8 or wider, so the CPU could run the detector in FP32 — a different operating point, fully measured, and therefore quotable. At E = 88.8% in FP32, one core delivers 31.3 GOP/s, and the detector would need 19.9 CPU cores and 869 mW of datapath power — against 0.48 of a tile and 106 mW.

That is the accelerator's case, in the only terms that matter to a silicon budget: 42 CPU cores' worth of work per KPU tile, at 8.2x less datapath energy. It is also the case for one tile rather than a large fabric: the catalogued T128 N7 part has 128 tiles, which is 269x more fabric than this mission can use. The accelerator earns its place here at a scale of one tile; the rest of that part is being bought for some other mission.

Step 5: what actually binds, and what to do about it

The sized configuration is 4 CPU cores and 1 KPU tile. The CPU is at 79% and the KPU at 48%, so the CPU is the part with no margin — and it is carrying 0.8% of the mission's operations to do it.

The cause is the 4.48% efficiency, not the core count. A per-pixel fixed-function chain on a general-purpose core spends its time on loads, stores and address arithmetic rather than on the 40 operations per pixel that the model counts. Three things would move it, in descending order of leverage:

  1. Put the light path in fixed-function silicon. The composed SoC already contains an ISP block, and this stage's own configuration note says demosaic and denoise are assumed to happen in the sensor ISP — what remains on the CPU is the 40 op/pixel light path after that. The catalogue states no capability or area for the on-die ISP, so this analysis cannot price the move; it can only say that the block is there and idle.
  2. Widen the CPU's vector path or fix its memory behaviour. Going from 4.48% to a still-modest 10% would take the light path from 3.16 cores to 1.41, and the part would need 2 cores instead of 4.
  3. Give the fabric a schedule for it. pixel_fixed_function is one of eleven kernel classes the domain-flow model declares unschedulable on a regular mesh. If that is wrong — and a per-pixel chain is about as regular as work gets — then the KPU tile at 48% has room to absorb it and the CPU count falls out of the design.

Each of those is a different team's work, which is the point of stating the sizing this way: the number that binds names the owner.

7. What this rests on

Every figure on this page is one of four things, and the page says which:

  • Catalogue data. The mission profile, its rates, its per-call operation and byte counts, the SoC composition and the process node's energy per operation.
  • A measurement. The CPU efficiencies come from the orin_nano_measured_v1 table — real silicon, with the run that produced each figure cited in the table.
  • A ceiling. The KPU efficiency is the domain-flow model's upper bound, taken from the smallest catalogued core with a stated schedule (kpu_t64_core) so that the bound does not carry a DRAM constraint belonging to a much larger fabric. A ceiling bounds; it does not predict. The tile count derived from it is therefore a lower bound: no fewer than 0.48 tiles.
  • A gap. Named as such, never filled.

Silicon area. The KPU side can be priced: a least-squares line through the four catalogued N7 cores gives 0.1665 mm² per tile plus 0.335 mm² fixed, so a one-tile fabric is about 0.50 mm². That is an extrapolation one step below the smallest catalogued SKU (64 tiles) and is labelled THEORETICAL for that reason. The CPU side cannot be priced at all: the repository states cache areas for the cluster but marks core logic unanchored, because no trustworthy absolute Cortex-A78AE core area is public. Arm discloses only relative figures. This is the one number in the whole dossier that a CPU IP partner could close immediately, and closing it would turn the area column from a gap into an answer.
