Mission dossier · kpu_t128_n7 on tsmc_n7

Humanoid: Cobot (human-adjacent, contact-rich)

Human pose and intent prediction, contact scheduling, 1 kHz safety filter. 16 stages, 90 W, 60 ms. What has to be true of the CPU and the accelerator for that to close.

This design does not serve this mission. It is short of 40 cores against 12. What it has is enough elsewhere: 18 tiles against 22 and memory at 64% of the interface. Two stages cannot be priced at all, so the engine and power figures here are floors -- the memory figure is not, since it counts every stage's bytes.

The shortfall has one owner: tsdf is 30.9% of the CPU demand.

1. The use case

A 30-degree-of-freedom humanoid working alongside people and touching things: predicting human pose and intent, scheduling contact, and running a 1000 Hz safety filter over 24 tracked hazards. There is no cage and no light curtain — the separation between the robot and a person is maintained in software, on this SoC, every millisecond.

That makes it the one mission in the catalogue where latency is not a performance property. A late frame on a warehouse AMR is a slower robot; a late frame here is a person struck by a 30-joint arm. The 60 ms deadline on the sense-to-act chain is the whole specification.

4 x 1280x720 streams at 30 Hz. 16 stages over 7 tiers, 11 of them on the sense-to-act chain that the 60 ms deadline applies to.

3. The workload

16 stages, from the sensor front end through state estimation, mapping and semantics to reactive control and actuation. They do not run at one rate: from 5 /s to 111 M/s in their own units, which is why each box carries what it demands of an engine rather than a share of some common frame.

The arithmetic is concentrated. det alone is 46% of the operations. The bytes are concentrated somewhere else: vla is 46% of the traffic. Those two facts pull the design in different directions, and section 6 is about which one wins.

5. The configuration

One KPU fabric, a cluster of Andes RISC-V cores, a shared fabric and one LPDDR5 interface at tsmc_n7. The capacities below — how many cores and tiles exist — come from the selected design and are an input. What section 6 produces is how many the mission needs, which is the number to compare them against.

The CPU block carries baseline figures: we have measured an Arm Cortex-A78AE, not an Andes core. They stand in for the socket until you replace them, which is what section 6 asks for. Nothing here is a claim about Andes silicon.

Three numbers per compute block, because any two mislead: X, the throughput one server actually delivers; U, the share of wall clock it is busy; E, the ops-weighted mean of the per-class efficiencies it runs at. X and U compose exactly — demand = X × servers × U — while E says how well the kernels suit the machine. Where a stage needs many servers at a low ceiling and another needs few at a high one, E and X/peak diverge, and E is the one that describes the kernels rather than the sizing.

A U above 100% is not a sizing. It is the design being asked for more than it has, and the figure beside it says by how much.

2. Size each engine

KPU. 4 stages need 18 tiles between them, against the 22 this design has. vla alone is 55.8% of that, at an efficiency of 65.31%.

CPU. 10 stages need 40 cores between them, against the 12 this design has. tsdf alone is 30.9% of that, at an efficiency of 0.34%.

3. What binds

  • the CPU: 334% — over capacity.
  • the KPU: 82% of what is there.
  • memory bandwidth: 64% of what is there.
  • power: 4% of what is there.

Note what that traffic is made of: 34% of it comes from stages no engine can price — sgm and vio. Bytes are counted whether or not an engine can run the work, so the memory figure is complete where the engine figures are floors, and 34% of the memory demand comes from stages the compute numbers leave out.

Memory is worth spelling out. The interface carries 131 GB/s of compulsory traffic against 204.8 GB/s of peak, and vla is 46% of it on its own — at 20 Hz, a 3.02 GB working set pulled through DRAM on every call.

4. The CPU ask

The CPU needs 40 cores and the design has 12. The gap is not spread evenly: three of the 10 stages on it are 86% of the demand.

  • tsdf (raycast): 12 cores at 0.34% of FP32 peak.
  • esdf (wavefront): 11 cores at 2.97% of FP32 peak.
  • mpc (small_qp): 11 cores at 12.92% of FP32 peak.

Grouped by kernel class, the answer is blunter still: raycast is 12 of the 40 cores (31%) at 0.34% of peak. So the question for an Andes core is not a general one:

What does it take to run 1.48 GOP/s of raycast in FP32 at materially better than 0.34% of peak?

The leverage is enormous because the baseline is so low. At 10% of peak instead of 0.34%, that class falls from 12 cores to 0.421, and the whole CPU requirement from 40 to 28 — 34 cores provisioned instead of 12.

5. What the accelerator is worth

det has no measured INT8 figure on a CPU, but its INT8 class may run in any wider format and FP32 is measured. At E = 88.8% one core delivers 31.3 GOP/s, so it would need 269 CPU cores and 11789 mW — against 6.46 of a tile and 3503 mW. That is 42 cores of work per tile at 3.4x less energy.

It is equally an argument for one tile: the catalogued part has 128 tiles, 7x more fabric than this mission can use.

Sizing answers a rate: can the machine do this much work per second. A safety argument needs a deadline: does each loop on the sense-to-act chain close inside its own period. Those are different questions, and this page answers only the first. The table below is the closest it gets to the second.

The filter closes; its inputs do not. The 1000 Hz cbf filter takes 161 us of its 1 ms period and needs 0.161 of a core. That part is comfortable. What is not is esdf at 11.2x its period and mpc at 11x its period.

cbf reads its hazards out of the ESDF, so at these rates it is correct arithmetic over a distance field that is 11.2x out of date.

A server count does not fix either one. Their units are per map update and per solve: the call is the loop iteration, so k servers carry k overlapping iterations rather than one faster one. That is a latency result, not a throughput result.

Two stages on the chain have no figure at all: sgm and vio. Nothing prices them on either engine, so they contribute no time to the table above. The chain's true latency is longer than anything here by an unknown amount, which is why this page quotes no end-to-end number.

What this model cannot tell you

Everything below is required for a safety case and absent here. None of it is a gap we can close by computing harder; each needs evidence this repository does not hold.

  • Worst-case execution time. Every figure on this page is a mean rate from measured throughput. A deadline is met or missed in the tail, and we model no tail: no cache behaviour, no contention, no interrupt latency, no scheduler.
  • Freedom from interference. The chain shares one memory interface with everything else, and the largest other consumers are vla at 60.4 GB/s and det at 17.8 GB/s — 38% of the interface between them. Nothing here bounds what that contention does to a period on the chain.
  • The safety island cannot be sized. The design carries one (island_sram anchored, lockstep_r52_cores not), but the catalogue states no compute capability for it, so no stage can be placed on it and this page cannot tell you what could run there instead.
  • Integrity level, fault coverage, diagnostic coverage, FIT rates and safe-state transition time. The catalogue carries none of these, so this page makes no claim about any standard, and its numbers cannot be cited towards one.

Read the table as a necessary condition, not a sufficient one, and read it against the efficiencies in it. A loop that does not close here does not close at these efficiencies — a faster implementation of the same kernel could close it, which is exactly what section 6 asks for. A loop that closes here has passed only the easiest of the tests it has to pass.

What this rests on

Four kinds of figure, and the page says which each is:

  • Catalogue. The mission, its rates, per-call operation and byte counts, the SoC composition, the node's energy per operation.
  • Measured. CPU efficiencies from orin_nano_measured_v1, with the run behind each figure cited in the table. These are measured on Arm Cortex-A78AE cores in a Jetson Orin Nano — our reference baseline, not a claim about any other core. They are why the CPU requirement is stated as a throughput target rather than a core count.
  • Ceiling. KPU efficiencies are our domain-flow upper bound, taken from the smallest core with a stated schedule (kpu_t64_core). A ceiling bounds, it does not predict, so every tile count is a lower bound.
  • Gap. Named, never filled.

Area. A least-squares line through our four catalogued N7 cores gives 0.1665 mm² per tile plus 0.287 mm² fixed, so a one-tile fabric is about 0.45 mm². The CPU side we cannot price at all: we anchor the cluster's caches but not its core logic, because no absolute core area is published for the baseline. An Andes core area would close that column outright.
