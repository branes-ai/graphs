Mission dossier · kpu_t128_n7 on tsmc_n7

AMR: Logistics (mixed / dynamic yard)

Humans and vehicles present, indoor-outdoor transitions, 4 m/s. 17 stages, 40 W, 120 ms. What has to be true of the CPU and the accelerator for that to close.

This design does not serve this mission. It is short of 27 cores against 12. What it has is enough elsewhere: 3.82 tiles against 5 and memory at 17% of the interface. Three stages cannot be priced at all, so the engine and power figures here are floors -- the memory figure is not, since it counts every stage's bytes.

The shortfall has one owner: tsdf is 57.2% of the CPU demand.

1. The use case

A logistics AMR working a mixed yard: indoor-outdoor transitions, humans and vehicles moving around it, no fiducials to lean on, 4 m/s. The same vehicle class as a warehouse AMR and very nearly the same pipeline — what differs is that nothing about the environment is known in advance.

3 x 1280x720 streams at 30 Hz. 17 stages over 7 tiers, 11 of them on the sense-to-act chain that the 120 ms deadline applies to.

3. The workload

17 stages, from the sensor front end through state estimation, mapping and semantics to reactive control and actuation. They do not run at one rate: from 3 /s to 82.9 M/s in their own units, which is why each box carries what it demands of an engine rather than a share of some common frame.

The arithmetic is concentrated. det alone is 80% of the operations. The bytes are concentrated somewhere else: sgm is 62% of the traffic. Those two facts pull the design in different directions, and section 6 is about which one wins.

No end-to-end latency is quoted. 3 stage(s) on this pipeline have no efficiency figure on either engine, so any chain total would silently omit them. The deadline cannot be checked until those are measured.

5. The configuration

One KPU fabric, a cluster of Andes RISC-V cores, a shared fabric and one LPDDR5 interface at tsmc_n7. Engine counts are the output of section 6, not an input.

The CPU block carries baseline figures: we have measured an Arm Cortex-A78AE, not an Andes core. They stand in for the socket until you replace them, which is what section 6 asks for. Nothing here is a claim about Andes silicon.

Three numbers per compute block, because any two mislead: X, the throughput one server actually delivers; U, the share of wall clock it is busy; E, the share of its dense peak that X represents. They compose exactly — demand = X × servers × U.

A U above 100% is not a sizing. It is the design being asked for more than it has, and the figure beside it says by how much.

2. Size each engine

KPU. 3 stages need 3.82 tiles between them, against the 5 this design has. det alone is 81.7% of that, at an efficiency of 97.30%.

CPU. 11 stages need 27 cores between them, against the 12 this design has. tsdf alone is 57.2% of that, at an efficiency of 0.34%.

3. What binds

  • the CPU: 226% — over capacity.
  • the KPU: 76% of what is there.
  • memory bandwidth: 17% of what is there.
  • power: 2% of what is there.

Memory is worth spelling out. The interface carries 35 GB/s of compulsory traffic against 204.8 GB/s of peak, and sgm is 11% of the interface on its own — at 30 Hz. That is a 728 MB working set pulled through DRAM on every call.

4. The CPU ask

The CPU needs 27 cores and the design has 12. The gap is not spread evenly: three of the 11 stages on it are 87% of the demand.

  • tsdf (raycast): 15 cores at 0.34% of FP32 peak.
  • esdf (wavefront): 5.97 cores at 2.97% of FP32 peak.
  • mono (pixel_fixed_function): 2.1 cores at 4.48% of FP32 peak.

Grouped by kernel class, the answer is blunter still: raycast is 17 of the 27 cores (61%) at 0.34% of peak. So the question for an Andes core is not a general one:

What does it take to run 1.98 GOP/s of raycast in FP32 at materially better than 0.34% of peak?

The leverage is enormous because the baseline is so low. At 10% of peak instead of 0.34%, that class falls from 17 cores to 0.564, and the whole CPU requirement from 27 to 11 — 14 cores provisioned instead of 12.

5. What the accelerator is worth

det has no measured INT8 figure on a CPU, but its INT8 class may run in any wider format and FP32 is measured. At E = 88.8% one core delivers 31.3 GOP/s, so it would need 130 CPU cores and 5691 mW — against 3.12 of a tile and 847 mW. That is 42 cores of work per tile at 6.7x less energy.

It is equally an argument for one tile: the catalogued part has 128 tiles, 34x more fabric than this mission can use.

The safety path

Sizing answers a rate: can the machine do this much work per second. A safety argument needs a deadline: does each loop on the sense-to-act chain close inside its own period. Those are different questions, and this page answers only the first. The table below is the closest it gets to the second.

The filter closes; an input it reads does not. The 200 Hz cbf filter takes 125 us of its 5 ms period and needs 0.0251 of a core. That part is comfortable. What is not is esdf at 5.97x its period.

cbf reads its hazards out of the ESDF, so at these rates it is correct arithmetic over a distance field that is 5.97x out of date.

A server count does not fix it. Its unit is per map update: the call is the loop iteration, so k servers carry k overlapping iterations rather than one faster one. That is a latency result, not a throughput result.

Two stages on the chain have no figure at all: sgm and vio. Nothing prices them on either engine, so they contribute no time to the table above. The chain's true latency is longer than anything here by an unknown amount, which is why this page quotes no end-to-end number.

What this model cannot tell you

Everything below is required for a safety case and absent here. None of it is a gap we can close by computing harder; each needs evidence this repository does not hold.

  • Worst-case execution time. Every figure on this page is a mean rate from measured throughput. A deadline is met or missed in the tail, and we model no tail: no cache behaviour, no contention, no interrupt latency, no scheduler.
  • Freedom from interference. The chain shares one memory interface with everything else, and the largest other consumers are det at 7.67 GB/s and tsdf at 596 MB/s — 4% of the interface between them. Nothing here bounds what that contention does to a period on the chain.
  • The safety island cannot be sized. The design carries one (island_sram anchored, lockstep_r52_cores not), but the catalogue states no compute capability for it, so no stage can be placed on it and this page cannot tell you what could run there instead.
  • Integrity level, fault coverage, diagnostic coverage, FIT rates and safe-state transition time. The catalogue carries none of these, so this page makes no claim about any standard, and its numbers cannot be cited towards one.

Read the table as a necessary condition, not a sufficient one: a loop that does not close here will not close on real silicon, but a loop that closes here has only passed the easiest of the tests it has to pass.

What this rests on

Four kinds of figure, and the page says which each is:

  • Catalogue. The mission, its rates, per-call operation and byte counts, the SoC composition, the node's energy per operation.
  • Measured. CPU efficiencies from orin_nano_measured_v1, with the run behind each figure cited in the table. These are measured on Arm Cortex-A78AE cores in a Jetson Orin Nano — our reference baseline, not a claim about any other core. They are why the CPU requirement is stated as a throughput target rather than a core count.
  • Ceiling. KPU efficiencies are our domain-flow upper bound, taken from the smallest core with a stated schedule (kpu_t64_core). A ceiling bounds, it does not predict, so every tile count is a lower bound.
  • Gap. Named, never filled.

Area. A least-squares line through our four catalogued N7 cores gives 0.1665 mm² per tile plus 0.287 mm² fixed, so a one-tile fabric is about 0.45 mm². The CPU side we cannot price at all: we anchor the cluster's caches but not its core logic, because no absolute core area is published for the baseline. An Andes core area would close that column outright.
